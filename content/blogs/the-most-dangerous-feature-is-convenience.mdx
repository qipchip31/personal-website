# the most dangerous feature is convenience

Every successful product promises the same thing.

Fewer clicks. Faster workflows. Less friction.

Most of the time, that is exactly what users want.

The problem is that convenience rarely removes work. It usually removes the moment when someone stops to think.

Auto-fill saves time until it fills the wrong account number. One-click purchases eliminate checkout until someone buys something by mistake. Automatic sharing makes collaboration easier until a private document becomes public.

The feature works exactly as designed. The mistake happens because the design removed the pause that once caught it.

Convenience is powerful because it turns repeated actions into habits. Habits are efficient, but they are also automatic. The less attention an action requires, the easier it becomes to perform it at the wrong time.

This is especially true in security.

The safest systems are not the ones with the most warnings. They are the ones that know which actions deserve a second thought. Most actions should feel effortless. A few should feel intentionally difficult.

There is a reason deleting an account, transferring money, or changing recovery settings often requires extra confirmation. The friction is not a flaw. It is part of the feature.

Good products remove unnecessary effort.

Great products know which effort should never disappear.
